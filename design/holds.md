# How a person holds each tool

The figure has one visible hand, the right, and that hand is a fist. These notes say where the fist closes, which way the working end points relative to the forearm and the ground, and whether the arm hangs, reaches forward, or is raised toward the face. A power grip is the whole hand wrapped around a handle, fingers toward the palm. A pinch or tripod grip holds the tool between the thumb and the fingertips. Where the documented grip is a pinch, the fist is marked as an adaptation: same place on the object, same aim, closed hand instead of the fingers.

The drawings in `design/prototype/tools` are a pose sheet for a first-pass prototype. They are not evidence of a hold, and nothing here is taken from them.

## Wrench

A wrench is held in one hand. The hand wraps the handle, away from the jaw. The jaw sits flat and fully on the nut or the bolt, not tilted. On an open-end or box wrench the jaw is at the end of the handle. On a common adjustable wrench the jaw is set about 30 degrees off the handle, and on a monkey wrench the head is at a right angle to the handle. The wrist stays straight, so the handle runs along the forearm, and the jaw points away from the fist onto the fastener. The hand pulls the handle toward the body with a slow, steady pull. A closed hand does not push. The arm reaches forward toward the work. It does not hang, and it is not raised to the face. If the fastener is overhead, the instruction is to stand aside rather than reach up under the wrench.

**Arm:** forward.

## Pencil

Writing is a one-handed precision grip, not a power grip. In the dynamic tripod, the usual adult hold, the pencil sits between the tip of the thumb and the end of the index finger and rests against the side of the middle finger. A lateral tripod, thumb crossed over the shaft, is the next grip adults actually use. The fingers close a short way back from the tip; closer to the tip gives more control. The tip points down at the page. The shaft runs back into the web between thumb and index finger. The wrist stays near neutral, not sharply bent, and the elbow is bent about a right angle, so the forearm reaches forward over the page. The hold is loose. The movement comes from the whole arm, not from a squeezed fist.

A fist cannot make a tripod. The adaptation is a fist closed on the shaft a short way back from the tip, tip still down at the work, shaft running back along the forearm, arm still reaching forward.

**Arm:** forward.

## Magnifying glass

A hand magnifier is a lens on a handle. The hand holds the handle, under the lens, not the glass. The lens is kept parallel to the object and at the lens’s focal distance from it, and the eye looks through the centre. For a page, the documented method is to set the magnifier on the page and bring it slowly toward the eye until the image is clear. Closer to the eye shows more words. Either the magnifier moves across the page, or the head and the magnifier stay put and the page moves. In that pose the arm reaches forward, the handle hangs below the lens, and the lens faces down at the page. The manufacturer’s training start is the other pose: the magnifier is held against the spectacles, the object is held against the lens, and the object is then pulled away until it is in focus. That raises the arm toward the face, with the lens facing out from the eye. The arm does not hang. A fist can close on the handle in either pose.

**Arm:** forward over a page, or raised to the eye.

- [Eschenbach Optik, Low Vision Training Module 3: Hand-held Magnifiers](https://eschenbach.com/images/Module%203%20Hand%20Held%20Magnifiers%20ver.%202.0%202016.pdf)
- [Manchester Royal Eye Hospital, Your Hand Magnifier](https://mft.nhs.uk/app/uploads/sites/2/2018/05/REH-035-Your-Hand-Magnifier.pdf)

## Brush

A small paintbrush is a precision tool. The manufacturer’s demonstration uses a pencil-style grip for a detailed stroke, a loose vertical hold for a straight stroke, and a hold with the fingers over the handle and the thumb underneath. The bristles are the working end. They meet the paper, so they point down at the work, not back along the forearm and not up at the face. Hand-therapy advice uses a paintbrush as the picture of a delicate hold: rest the tool in the fingers, do not squeeze it. The arm reaches forward over the work.

A fist is an adaptation of those finger grips. It closes on the handle, never on the bristles. The overhand hold, brush vertical and bristles down, is the one a closed hand can resemble. A pencil-style grip on the brush is the same adaptation as the pencil: fist a short way up the handle, bristles down, arm forward.

**Arm:** forward.

- [Ashford and St Peter’s Hospitals, Hand Therapy, Handwriting Advice](https://www.ashfordstpeters.info/images/leaflets/hand-therapy/HN122-Hand%20Therapy-Handwriting-Advice-Jun-2026.pdf)
- [CCOHS, Hand Tool Ergonomics — Tool Design](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/ergonomics/handtools/tooldesign.html)

## Clipboard

The clinical instruction is to use a clipboard, or a paperweight, so the palm does not have to hold the paper. The same leaflet puts the writing elbow at about a right angle, the forearm reaching forward, and the wrist neutral. That describes the writing arm, not a hand closed on the board. No occupational-safety page or manufacturer page reviewed here says where a hand closes on a clipboard, or which way a carried board points. A one-fist hold would be an adaptation of an object whose documented job is to free the hand, and the grip point is not sourced. This note does not invent one. The page faces the reader when the board is in use.

**Arm:** not documented for a held board. The writing arm reaches forward.

- [Ashford and St Peter’s Hospitals, Hand Therapy, Handwriting Advice](https://www.ashfordstpeters.info/images/leaflets/hand-therapy/HN122-Hand%20Therapy-Handwriting-Advice-Jun-2026.pdf)

## Watering can

The can this maker still builds is two-handed. It has a lifting handle and a separate tilting handle at the rear. The older can it replaced had one looped handle over the top, and that single handle both lifted and tilted. Pouring is a tip. A steeper tip pours faster, and the rose faces downward so the water goes to the soil rather than onto the leaves. The spout points down toward the ground.

The one-hand adaptation is that older top handle, which is also the only grip a single fist can use. The fist closes on the loop over the top. The body of the can hangs below the hand. The arm reaches forward and tips the can until the spout points down. A can that only hangs does not pour, and the arm is not raised to the face.

**Arm:** forward.

- [Haws, The history of Haws](https://haws.co.uk/pages/140-years-of-haws)
- [Haws, Watering technique](https://haws.co.uk/pages/watering-technique-getting-the-best-from-your-can)

## Telescope

A spotting scope is usually mounted on a tripod. Smaller ones can be held. Binoculars, two small telescopes side by side, are the documented handheld grip: both hands bring them up to the eyes, thumbs under the barrels and fingers on top, elbows rested against the chest. The eye sits just behind the eyepiece, not pressed against the glass. Two hands, or a tripod, is how the maker says to keep the view steady.

The one-hand adaptation, for a small telescope when the other hand is busy, is one fist wrapped around the tube, the eyepiece raised to the eye and held just off it, and the far end pointing away roughly level with the ground. The arm is raised toward the face. It is a less steady hold than the one the maker asks for.

**Arm:** raised.

- [Celestron, The Ultimate Guide to Observing Land-Based Objects](https://www.celestron.com/blogs/knowledgebase/the-ultimate-guide-to-observing-land-based-objects)
- [Celestron, How do I use my telescope eyepieces?](https://www.celestron.com/blogs/knowledgebase/how-do-i-use-my-telescope-eyepieces)

## Hammer

A hammer is held in one hand. The fingers wrap the handle. The handle is straight: the blow is a vertical force, and a straight handle is the one paired with a vertical force and a straight wrist. The handle is long enough to cross the whole palm, so the butt does not dig into the palm. The fist closes on the handle, the head is on the far side of the fist, and the butt sits at or just past the heel of the hand. The striking face points down at the work and meets it square, parallel to the surface, not with the cheek of the head. The swing needs room behind and above, so the arm is raised to start. At the blow the forearm is out in front, the wrist is straight, and the face points down. The arm does not hang while striking, and it is not raised to the face.

**Arm:** raised to start the swing, forward at the blow.

## Saw

A hand saw is held in one hand. The other hand steadies the wood, or its thumb stands against the blade to start the cut. That hand is not on the saw. The fist passes through the handle. The handle opening asked for is at least 12 cm long and 6 cm wide, slanted about 15 degrees, so the wrist can stay natural in the horizontal plane. Pressure is on the downstroke, so the teeth point down at the cut. The stroke goes away from the body, not toward it. The blade leaves the fist pointing away, toothed edge toward the ground. The arm reaches forward. It does not hang, and it is not raised to the face.

**Arm:** forward.

- [CCOHS, Hand Tools — Hand Saw](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/hand_tools/handsaw.html)
- [CCOHS, General Hand Tool Operation](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/hand_tools/general.html)

## Fairy wand

A fairy wand has no safety standard and no maker’s grip instruction. The closest documented one-hand hold is a conductor’s baton. The evidence for the wand itself is thin.

The baton is held at the balance point, where the handle meets the shaft. It sits between the pad of the thumb and the first joint of the index finger, and the other fingers wrap it loosely. The handle only touches the palm. The palm faces the ground. The stick is about level with the ground and points away from the body, closer to straight out than to a steep diagonal. The tip leads the beat. The arm is raised in front of the body. The tip is not pointed at the floor, and the arm does not hang.

A fist is only a rough match. The documented hold is light, between thumb and index finger, not a power grip. The fist can close on the handle at that balance point, with the stick leaving the fist pointing forward, roughly level.

**Arm:** raised.

- [Amy Kotsonis, The Baton, in Music in Motion, University of New Hampshire](https://pressbooks.pub/musicinmotion/chapter/chapter-4-the-baton/)

## Scissors

The scissors are held in one hand. The other hand holds the paper and turns it, so the blades can keep pointing forward. The thumb goes through the upper bow, thumb up. A finger goes through the lower bow, and the index finger may stay outside that bow to steady it. The wrist is straight or slightly bent back. The blades point away from the body, not across the front of it. The hand works at least 8 to 12 inches from the face, the upper arm stays against the side, and the elbow can rest on the table. The arm reaches forward. It is not raised to the face, and it does not hang.

A fist cannot occupy the two bows. The adaptation is a fist closed around both bows, thumb-side up, blades still pointing away, arm still forward.

**Arm:** forward.

- [Poole Hospital NHS Foundation Trust, Children’s Therapy Services, Scissor Skills](https://primarysite-prod-sorted.s3.amazonaws.com/swanageprimaryswanage/UploadedDocument/93cf54281c544661b45f633d813e58a0/scissor-skills.pdf)
- [Liverpool Children’s Occupational Therapy Service, Scissor Skills](https://primarysite-prod-sorted.s3.amazonaws.com/millstead-special-needs-school/UploadedDocument/3e0422b1-6035-4c7d-981c-abf77fb34a66/developing_scissor_skills.pdf)

## Tongs

Kitchen tongs are a one-handed tool with two handles, in the same class as pliers. One hand spans both handles and squeezes them together. The separation asked for between those handles is about 65 to 90 mm. The fist closes across the two handles at the hinge end, not on the tips. The tips are the working end and point away from the fist. The use position given for a hand tool of this sort is pointing down, so over a pan or a counter the tips point down and the arm reaches forward. The arm is not raised to the face. No cookery manual was found that states this pose; the downward aim is the general tool-use position, not a kitchen instruction.

**Arm:** forward.

- [CCOHS, Hand Tool Ergonomics — Tool Design](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/ergonomics/handtools/tooldesign.html)

## Weakly sourced

The fairy wand has no standard of its own. The baton page is a conducting textbook, and its grip is a light finger hold. A closed fist is a rough stand-in.

The clipboard is the thinnest of the set. The hand-therapy leaflet uses the board so the palm does not grip the paper. It never says where a hand closes on the board.

The telescope’s steady hold is two hands, or a tripod. One fist on the tube is an adaptation, and the maker treats one-handed viewing as the shaky case.

The pencil, the brush, and the scissors are finger grips. The fist positions above keep the working end aimed the way the sources describe, but they are not the grips those sources teach.

The watering can in the Haws design is two-handed. The single top handle is the older one-hand pattern, used here because the figure has one hand.

The hammer notes do not say to choke up at the butt for leverage. No primary page reviewed here says that. What they do say is that the handle crosses the whole palm, and that the face meets the work square and pointing down.

The tongs’ tips-down aim comes from the general rule that a hand tool is held pointing down in use. It is not from a kitchen manual.
